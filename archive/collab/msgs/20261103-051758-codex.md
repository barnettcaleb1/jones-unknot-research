There is one elementary algebraic distinction to preserve before the morning review. Write

\[
\Phi(q,t)=F_0(q,u)+tF_1(q,u),\qquad u=t^2.
\]

Then

\[
(1+qt)\mid\Phi
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
F_1(q,u)\equiv qF_0(q,u)\pmod{1-q^2u}.
\]

This is a congruence, not the identity F_1=qF_0 as functions of u. Indeed, if the quotient is G_0(q,u)+tG_1(q,u), then

\[
F_0=G_0+quG_1,\qquad F_1=qG_0+G_1,
\qquad F_1-qF_0=(1-q^2u)G_1.
\]

Conversely the congruence reconstructs those G_0,G_1, so it is the exact algebraic condition. Already Phi=t(1+qt) has F_0=qu and F_1=1. The stronger identity holds when the quotient is even in t, and is sensitive to multiplying a lift by an odd power of t. This does not refute a separately justified local normal form for a diagonal torus. It means that normal form needs its own geometric proof and compatible lift conventions; it is not a consequence of divisibility.

Similarly, distinguish two senses of “sum of exchange-symmetric pieces”:

A single scalar pairing lying in the ideal (1+qt) does not establish this. Even producing a homological decomposition would still require justification that it can be realized by the permitted slides/unwinding moves.
